Opening · 1–2 minutes

Lumen has two purposes. Retrospective MTA explains observed conversion histories and allocates reporting credit. Prospective targeting asks which action changes a customer's future outcome. A prediction of who will convert does not alone answer the second question.

The Layer 6 conversation began with CLTV and moved toward a research proof of concept using available data and causal meta-learners. Today we are proposing a campaign benchmark using Lumen's data, rather than committing to a production model.

Plan for about 10–12 minutes across the seven main slides and the rest of the 30-minute meeting for discussion. The objective is agreement on one useful benchmark and the data improvements needed to support it.

Talk track · 2 minutes

MTA starts with customer history and predicts conversion. We then examine channel or finer-group contributions and apply an explicit credit rule for reporting. The outputs answer what the model attributes to the observed history.

The current user-reported workflow uses XGBoost with group-specific monotonic constraints, counts, recency and decay features. Explanations include group SHAP, TreeSHAP and group removal. These methods and the credit conventions are not interchangeable. There is no need to discuss those implementation choices unless asked.

Positive-only, negative-residual and signed-net credit are comparison options, not a single selected normalization. No weights in this diagram are measured. It is a conceptual process diagram, with no claim of causal correctness or improved results.

The incumbent selects one interaction through linkage, eligibility, lookback and ranking rules. Fractional MTA distributes credit through an explicit allocation convention. Neither requires that credit equal incremental conversion lift.

Talk track · 2 minutes

Both panels use invented numbers. The left is an illustrative probability-scale additive explanation: a reference prediction of 10% plus 6 percentage points attributed to the email feature group and 4 to the web group yields a 20% prediction. This explains the model relative to its reference. It does not say sending an email increases conversion by six points. Actual explanations may be on another model-output scale; do not mix log-odds SHAP values with probabilities.

The right is a separate population-level randomized example: 12% conversion under assignment to an email policy versus 10% under holdout gives a two-point average assignment effect. Y is conversion in the same follow-up window and assignment is randomized within the relevant strata. This is not an individual counterfactual observation. The two panels have different targets and are not a numerical decomposition of the same effect.

Retrospective causal MTA would ask how conversions would change under a specified intervention on a contact or sequence. Removing a feature from a predictive model does not create that intervention. Channel effects can interact and need not sum to a conversion; allocating causal effects into reporting credit still requires an explicit rule. The current allocation conventions remain under comparison; this slide does not choose one.

Talk track · 2 minutes

This is a candidate, not an agreed experiment. Begin with one product and a well-documented campaign/wave, preferably intended email-only cells and their comparable holdouts. Define the outcome horizon together with Layer 6 rather than imposing an arbitrary window. Keep everyone assigned, including undelivered and nonresponding customers.

Y(1) and Y(0) refer to assignment to this campaign contact policy or its holdout. τ is the conditional average incremental conversion probability, not an individual observed causal label. Other campaigns remain part of the background policy unless explicitly handled. Randomization must be valid within the chosen strata and analysis population.

Use stratified randomized mean differences as an aggregate anchor. Compare boosted-tree T-learner and cross-fitted DR-learner with CausalPFN on the same cohort and baseline features. Add lagged PRISM scores if available before assignment; they predict acquisition and are not treatment-assignment probabilities.

Keep model selection separate from evaluation. Hold out customers and test a later wave when feasible. Use actual randomization probabilities, account for repeated customers or cluster assignment, and report uncertainty. At a fixed contact budget compare learned uplift ranking with existing/PRISM and random targeting. Do not use AUC alone to select the causal policy. If dollars enter the objective, align outcome value and contact costs.

CausalPFN's published implementation is binary, so it fits this first benchmark more directly than a full adaptive multi-channel policy. A static baseline benchmark does not establish which channel is best at every later decision point.

Talk track · 2 minutes

First, cells can bundle channels. A randomized bundle-versus-holdout contrast can be valid for that bundle. It does not automatically isolate an email or mobile component. Different bundles may support additional contrasts if the design and common support justify them. A single-channel cell also does not guarantee no other-campaign contact.

Second, overlap is not automatically fatal. If other campaigns are independently assigned and unaffected by this assignment, the contrast averages over that background policy. If contact caps or prioritization cause rerouting, the effect includes those pathways. Do not casually exclude or adjust for later contacts that the original assignment could have caused.

Third, prompt TARGETED records can reflect queues or eligibility. Branch also depends on customer visit and advisor action. For the first benchmark, keep randomized assignment as the treatment. Actual receipt/viewing is a different causal question with additional assumptions.

Finally, freeze the feature snapshot before assignment. The deck says email consent is often absent and mostly current-only. This is a limitation for reconstructing eligible actions and deploying a policy; it does not by itself prove a historical randomized contrast invalid. Current flags cannot simply be used as historical features.

A minimal benchmark specification

Design

Unit
Customer × campaign assignment occasion

Treatment
Assigned contact policy versus holdout

Outcome
Product conversion within a common horizon

Baseline
Only information available before assignment

Evaluation

Aggregate anchor
Randomized lift within assignment strata

Fair model comparison
Same cohort, features and outcome window

Independent assessment
Held-out customers and later-wave transfer

Decision metric
Lift at a fixed contact budget, with uncertainty

Begin with one eligible occasion per customer if that makes the first benchmark easier to audit, explicitly restricting its scope. Otherwise use splitting and uncertainty calculations that respect repeated customers and any cluster randomization. Keep fitting/tuning entirely separate from evaluation. PFN context must never contain evaluation outcomes.

When test fractions differ across strata, use the design probabilities and appropriate aggregation. A naive pooled test-control difference can be misleading. Outcome absence means nonconversion only when the horizon is observed and the relevant conversion stream is complete.

Compare T/DR learners with CausalPFN and simple aggregate/targeting baselines. For DR learning use assignment propensity, not product acquisition propensity. Report performance at matched sample sizes and investigate PFN scaling separately. No PEHE from real outcomes without counterfactual truth; semi-synthetic experiments are a distinct evidence layer.

For subsequent channel choice, define feasible actions and a common future contact policy. A useful target is E[Y(a, π₀) − Y(no contact, π₀) ∣ Hₜ]. Different channels' effects estimated in different eligible populations cannot simply be compared as though they were alternatives for the same customer.
